## In one sentence
Effective access is the union of every role assignment that reaches a scope, with any deny assignment overriding the result. Reading it means resolving that union — not hunting for a single winning rule.

> **Whole-picture reference:** the [RBAC complete view](../visuals/rbac-complete.html) shows the evaluation order (deny assignment first → roles → conditions) alongside every related concept.

## Why it exists
You already resolve effective rules for a living: an NSG hands you a pile of allow/deny entries and you work out what traffic actually passes. Azure RBAC is the same question — "given all these assignments, what can this principal do here?" — but the resolution logic is **different from a firewall**, and that difference is the whole objective.

An NSG has priority numbers and first-match-wins. Azure RBAC has **neither**. There is no order, no priority, no "most specific rule". RBAC is a flat **union of every allow**, plus a separate deny layer that always wins. If you carry your NSG instinct across unchanged, you will get these questions wrong.

## Mental model
Effective access = **(everything any role assignment grants, added together) minus (anything a deny assignment blocks)**. Deny is not a high-priority rule; it is a different, higher layer.

Scopes nest from broad to narrow, and assignments flow downward. **Check access** shows what reaches a scope from that scope and above — never what sits only at a child.

![Scope levels for Azure RBAC](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/includes/role-based-access-control/media/scope-levels.png){Management group > subscription > resource group > resource. An assignment at any level is inherited by everything below it.}

## What actually matters
- **Assignments are additive (a union).** Two roles on the same principal give the sum of both. There is no priority and no conflict to resolve between two allows — more assignments only ever mean more access.
- **A deny assignment beats every role assignment.** If a deny and an allow both apply, the deny wins. You cannot out-rank a deny with an Owner assignment.
- **You cannot create deny assignments yourself.** Azure creates and manages them — through deployment stacks, Azure Blueprints, or managed apps. Every deny assignment today is **system-protected** (read-only). They can **exclude** named principals and can be stopped from inheriting to child scopes.
- **`NotActions` is not a deny.** It subtracts actions *within one role definition* only. If role A excludes an action via `NotActions` but role B grants it, the union still grants it. Only a real deny assignment removes an action the union granted.
- **Inheritance is one-directional and additive.** An assignment at the subscription is effective on every resource group and resource under it. Check access and View my access list "this scope and inherited to this scope" — assignments that exist only at child scopes are **not** shown.
- **Azure RBAC roles and Microsoft Entra roles are separate.** Global Administrator does not grant Azure resource access by default; it must elevate access first. Don't read an Entra role as an Azure resource permission.

## Where it lives
- **Portal — one principal:** any scope (**Management group / Subscription / Resource group / resource**) > **Access control (IAM)** > **Check access** tab > **Check access** button > pick the user/group/service principal. Shows role assignments *and* deny assignments reaching that scope.
- **Portal — yourself at a scope:** **Access control (IAM)** > **Check access** > **View my access**.
- **Portal — yourself everywhere:** top-right **Profile** menu > **…** > **My permissions**.
- **Portal — the full list:** **Access control (IAM)** > **Role assignments** tab (rows marked **(Inherited)** come from a parent), **Roles** tab (built-in + custom), **Deny assignments** tab. **Download role assignments** exports the current inventory.
- **Audit who changed what:** **Subscription > Activity log**, filter Operation on `roleAssignments/write` and `roleAssignments/delete`. (Retained 90 days.)
- **CLI:** `az role assignment list --assignee {upn|objectId} --scope {scope} --include-inherited --include-groups` · `az role assignment list --all`.
- **PowerShell (Az.Resources):** `Get-AzRoleAssignment -Scope {scope}` or `-ObjectId {id}` · `Get-AzDenyAssignment` for deny assignments (there is no portal/CLI command to *create* one).

## Exam traps
- **"Two roles conflict — which wins?" is a trick.** Allows never conflict. The answer is the **union** of both. Do not pick the "more specific" or "lower scope" one.
- **Deny always wins — ==!even over Owner==.** A scenario with a deny assignment and an Owner role: the denied action is still blocked.
- **`NotActions` ≠ deny.** A distractor will say a role's `NotActions` "denies" an action for the user. It only removes it *from that role*; another role can still grant it.
- **Child-scope assignments are invisible in Check access.** Checking at a resource group will not reveal a role assigned directly on one resource inside it. The pane only reads this scope and above.
- **Eligible (PIM) is not active.** An eligible assignment grants nothing until the user activates it, and time-bound access only applies in its window. "Has the role eligible" does not mean "has access right now".
- **Identity not found / Unknown** in the list means a deleted principal (or replication lag), not a working assignment — orphaned, grants nothing.
- **Changes are not instant.** Role assignment changes can take up to ==@~10 minutes== to take effect; a new principal can hit replication delay. "Just assigned but still denied" is usually propagation, not a wrong assignment.
- **Entra Global Administrator ≠ Azure access.** It must elevate access first; by itself it does not grant resource-plane permissions.
- **Four independent gates can block an operation, not just RBAC.** An action can fail for a missing role, a deny assignment, a resource lock, or an Azure Policy Deny — any one is enough, and they are checked independently. See the "what blocked this action?" decision tree on **D1.3.1 (Azure Policy) > Exam traps**.
